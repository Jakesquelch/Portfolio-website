## Introduction

So what is a homelab/homelabbing? Homelabs can be used for lots of things,
specifically for me I'm interested in the idea of self-hosting. A homelab will
allow me to utilise a private, self-contained computer to run my own server.
Over the years I have subscribed to third-party cloud providers and services
such as Google Photos and it's got to a point where these have built up and are
now costing a bit more money than I would like, each month. More importantly, I
like the idea of owning my data myself, that is the real motivator that
triggered my excitement to learn about homelabbing.

## So what is my goal?

For now the goal is to create my own server where I can store my data and not
rely on these third-party companies to do so for me. Specifically, I am starting
with Google Photos which costs me a whopping £1.79 a month! I'm going to attempt
to replace Google Photos with [Immich](https://immich.app/) (a self-hosted,
open-source photo and video management solution) and thereby hopefully being
able to cancel the payments that I make for the service of Google Photos. This
is my current goal but I'm very flexible with this, it is more about learning
how this all works and whether this is a solution I think would actually work
for me.

## Getting started

There is masses of information out there regarding homelabs and it can be a bit
overwhelming when you start. I decided instead of going down the tutorial-hell
route and stuck in a procrastination cycle, I would just get started with an old
laptop that I didn't really use anymore, as that is all you need to get started.

### Setting up my laptop

I installed [Ubuntu Server](https://ubuntu.com/server) onto the laptop, I
decided to use this instead of Ubuntu Desktop because the specs for my laptop
aren't the best (11GB RAM & Celeron N5095 4c/4t) and I wanted less RAM and CPU
used as well as less software installed.

### Step 1 - Hardening

First thing I wanted to do was harden up my server and make sure it was secure.
This involves removing potential ways in. Bots continuously scan the internet
and anything with SSH open immediately gets `root`/`admin`/`ubuntu` tried
against the most common passwords, so this is what we need to defend against.

So these are some things I did to help harden my server:

- Password auth off, SSH keys only
- Root login disabled

Next I installed [`ufw`](https://help.ubuntu.com/community/UFW) which stands for
"Uncomplicated Firewall". It technically isn't actually a firewall it's more of
a wrapper but that's beside the point. This is a host firewall which means it
runs on and filters my laptops traffic only. It writes rules into my laptops
kernel and cannot reach the router. I picked `ufw` because it's Ubuntu's default
and handles IPv4 and IPv6 together without being asked. A firewall is important
to have because it blocks unwanted incoming traffic and stops potentially
compromised devices from reaching other services on your server (I have
default-deny inbound).

### Step 2 - Installing Infrastructure

I then decided to install [Docker](https://www.docker.com/) to solve dependency
hell problems that could and would likely occur in the future. For example
instead of installing Python packages system-wide as that is a dependency for
Immich, and then in the future a new service wanting a different version, and
the two colliding over one system-wide library. We can use Docker so that every
service has its own private complete filesystem with exactly the versions it
wants (this is containerisation). I also installed
[`docker compose`](https://docs.docker.com/compose/) because Immich is four
containers and I want to make it easy to bring them all up together.

[Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/) was up next, this is something I had heard
of before but had no clue what it did. Put simply it solves the home LAN
problem. My server is reachable from the home LAN and nowhere else, and I needed
a way to use Immich from my phone via mobile data or on another laptop when I'm
out and about. To achieve this, a connection has to reach my server which is
sitting behind a NAT on my home router. Tailscale solves that problem. Port
forwarding is an alternative option but I didn't fancy forwarding port `2283` to
the internet. Under the hood of Tailscale is
[WireGuard](https://www.wireguard.com/), but it also adds coordination on top of
it, which made things a lot easier for me. Tailscale creates a private network
of your devices (a tailnet), and then the devices make outbound connections to a
coordination server, and that is how my devices can now communicate to each
other.

### Step 3 - Installing Immich

Now I had my infrastructure in place it was time to actually install Immich and
transfer my photos over. Firstly, I used
[Google Takeout](https://takeout.google.com/) to export all my photos from
Google Photos. Next I installed Immich and
[`immich-go`](https://github.com/simulot/immich-go) to run the import of all my
photos. And just like that it was all done! I had successfully transferred all
of my google photos over to Immich. I had set up Tailscale and Immich
successfully on my phone so that I could view my photos there as well. Now this
is all well and good and you may be thinking we are done there...we are not.

## Final thoughts

Before I am able to delete my Google Photos subscription, I need to make sure
that my data is safe and secure. So those are the next steps. At the moment we
do not have backups of our photos, all our immich data sits on one SSD on my
laptop, if that drive dies, I would lose everything. A common rule to follow is
the [3-2-1 rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backup#3-2-1_Backup_Rule):

- 3 copies (this can help against just general bad luck such as a drive failing)
- 2 media types (to help prevent against correlated failures)
- 1 offsite (against fires, floods etc)
